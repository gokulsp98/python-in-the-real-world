Every ticket is a transaction.

It lands in an OLTP database.

OLTP — Online Transaction Processing. Built to write one record at a time, cheaply, and fetch it back instantly. "Get me ticket #4471" is exactly the job it's good at. MongoDB, Postgres, MySQL — the app runs on this, and runs on this fine.

Fast, cheap writes Instant single-row reads

Meanwhile, upstairs

Leadership doesn't want one ticket. They want the pattern across all of them.

How many payment issues this quarter? How much is product damage? How many are delayed deliveries? Get that right, and you can plan the next quarter instead of guessing — and what you're really building is user trust.

Every report is a question at scale.

Ask OLTP to group a million rows, and it chokes.

OLTP is built for "one row, right now" — not "scan everything and count." Run that aggregation on the same database your app depends on, and you either slow the app down too, or pay a lot of money for the computing power it takes. Neither is the plan.

The database built for this job

Enter OLAP — built to read everything at once.

OLAP — Online Analytical Processing. Think Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift. It's organized for the opposite trade-off: scanning and aggregating huge volumes fast. Writing one row at a time is the expensive part here — so you don't. You write in batches.

Built for aggregation reads Cheap only in batches

So where's the data leadership needs?

Sitting on the wrong side.

The ticket lives in OLTP. The query that actually works lives in OLAP. Someone has to move the data across — and at real company scale, millions of tickets, every team, every quarter, a simple script looping row by row just won't finish in time.

Python's answer to "too big for one script"

PySpark — Python, spread across many machines.

PySpark splits the job: read a huge slice of OLTP data, hand pieces to many workers in parallel, transform it, then write it out to OLAP in bulk batches — not row by row. Same Python you already know, just spread across many computers working together underneath.

Parallel, not row-by-row Built for big-data batch jobs
