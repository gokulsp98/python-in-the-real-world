A real-world product, before anything else

Picture an online marketplace for used cars.

A seller lists a car. A buyer browses listings. A deal happens, and the car changes hands — all without either of them visiting a dealership.

Every listing needs one thing first

What is this specific car actually worth, right now?

Not what it cost new. Not what a similar car sold for last year. What it is worth today — given its mileage, its condition, and a market that moves week to week. A human expert can answer that for one car in a few minutes. Thousands of new listings arrive every single day.

So how do you actually solve this?

Not with one script. With a pipeline.

A chain of specialized stages, each doing one job well. Let's walk it stage by stage — what happens there, and which tool does the work.

Stage 1 of 6

Collect

You can't predict a price out of thin air. The model has to learn from history: thousands of cars that already sold, and what they actually sold for — plus specs, condition reports, and current market activity, all pulled from databases and external sources.

Engineer Features

Raw facts aren't enough — they have to become numbers the model can actually learn from. A "feature" is one measurable fact about the car.

Stage 3 of 6 — training, part one

Think of it as a series of yes/no questions

Mileage > 50,000?
Older than 5 yrs?
≈ $7,400
Any damage?
≈ $9,100

A decision tree is exactly that: split the question, split again, and land on a price. One tree is simple — and on its own, not very accurate.

Stage 3 of 6 — training, part two

So use hundreds of small trees, not one

Tree 1
rough guess
Tree 2
fixes tree 1's errors
Tree 3
fixes tree 2's errors
Tree 300
final price

This is gradient boosting — like a team of specialists, where each new member's only job is to correct the mistakes the team made so far. Hundreds of weak trees, combined, make one strong predictor.

Stage 4 of 6

Evaluate

Before trusting a model, test it on sales it has never seen, and measure the average error. Not good enough? Adjust and retrain. Good enough? Ship it — then repeat this whole pipeline on a regular schedule (e.g. weekly) as new sales come in, so the model never goes stale.

Python ML Libraryscikit-learn metrics Python Visualization Librarymatplotlib

Stage 5 of 6

Deploy as an API

A trained model sitting in a file is useless to other software. So it gets wrapped in a small web service — other programs send a car's details as a request, and get a price back as a response. Crucially, it's the same language that trained the model, so nothing gets lost in translation on the way to production.

Python Web FrameworkFastAPI / Flask Containerization ToolDocker

Stage 6 of 6

Serve & Learn

A new listing arrives → its details hit the API → the model returns a price in milliseconds → a thin business-rules check sanity-checks it (e.g. never below a legal minimum) → the price appears on screen. Then, once the car actually sells, that outcome flows right back into Stage 1 — Collect. The loop closes. The model keeps learning.
